## Scope

“Planckian” commonly describes a specified relaxation rate on the scale $k_BT/\hbar$, with a dimensionless coefficient of order unity. It is not a theorem inferred from a resistivity curve that happens to be linear in temperature. Transport, optical, single-particle, thermalization and quantum-chaos times probe different correlation functions and need not be equal. [R1](#ref-source-67da95cac5481029)

A useful experimental statement names the channel, fitting range and assumptions. It also separates an empirical scale from a proposed universal bound. The phrase “of order one” must not become an exact $\alpha=1$ assertion without uncertainties and a consistent convention. [R1](#ref-source-67da95cac5481029) [R2](#ref-source-e6d342432f3409ae)

## Mathematical assumptions

For a specified transport lifetime, a total dimensionless ratio is

$
\alpha_{\mathrm{tot}}(T)=\frac{\hbar}{k_BT\,\tau_{\mathrm{tr}}(T)}.
$

A model with a residual rate can instead be parametrized as

$
\frac{1}{\tau_{\mathrm{tr}}(T)}
=\frac{1}{\tau_0}+\alpha_{\mathrm{inel}}\frac{k_BT}{\hbar}.
$

The second coefficient describes the inelastic slope under this assumption; it is not the first ratio when a residual term is appreciable. This distinction is made explicit in converting the source's ASCII notation. [R1](#ref-source-67da95cac5481029)

In a single-band Drude limit with temperature-independent effective inputs,

$
\rho=\frac{m^\ast}{ne^2\tau_{\mathrm{tr}}},\qquad
\alpha_{\mathrm{inel}}
=\frac{\hbar}{k_B}\frac{ne^2}{m^\ast}\frac{d\rho}{dT}.
$

The resistivity slope alone does not supply the carrier density $n$ or transport mass $m^\ast$. Multiband conduction, changing spectral weight, angular scattering and vertex effects can invalidate this simple inversion. Sheet resistance and bulk resistivity must also use consistent thickness conventions. [R1](#ref-source-67da95cac5481029) [R2](#ref-source-e6d342432f3409ae)

## Experimental criteria

Specify the lifetime definition, residual subtraction, frequency convention, fitting interval and uncertainties. State whether a quoted rate is an angular frequency or ordinary frequency; confusing $h$ with $\hbar$ introduces a factor of $2\pi$. Use independent optical or Fermi-surface constraints where available, and propagate their uncertainty into an inferred coefficient.

Bruin and colleagues compared inferred rates across metals, while Legros and colleagues examined overdoped cuprates with a per-plane scaling analysis. Those studies provide important constraints within their transport assumptions. They do not measure one exact universal coefficient for every composition and every relaxation channel. ## Limits and disputes

Maldacena, Shenker and Stanford bound the growth of a regularized out-of-time-order correlator by $\lambda_L\leq 2\pi k_BT/\hbar$. Their argument assumes analyticity and boundedness in a thermal complex-time strip, controlled factorization errors, and a separation between dissipation and scrambling times. It constrains a quantum-chaos exponent, not a measured charge-transport relaxation rate. Identifying $\lambda_L$ with $1/\tau_{\mathrm{tr}}$ requires additional model-specific justification; the bound does not establish a universal transport coefficient $\alpha=1$. A fitted normal-state channel and a superconducting gap are different evidence objects. [R4](#ref-source-05b2d5ff6757b5eb)

## Discovery implications

Use a Planckian-scale observation to formulate a falsifiable normal-state model. Ask which spectral weight, carrier estimate and scattering anisotropy would support or reject the proposed interpretation. Proximity to superconductivity can motivate a mechanism study, but it does not establish that faster scattering raises the critical temperature.
