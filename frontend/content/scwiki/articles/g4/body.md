## Scope

Reentrance describes a path through a phase diagram. Specify which phase returns, which parameter is swept, its direction, and what is held fixed. A field scan can produce superconducting, dissipative, and superconducting regimes in sequence. The historical term reentrant superconductor also covers a different sequence on cooling: normal, superconducting, then normal ferromagnetic. A pair of maxima in a transition-temperature curve does not by itself establish disconnected superconducting regions. [R1](#ref-source-435b794ed32c7604) [R4](#ref-source-9be3bcd270b306a2) [R6](#ref-source-e917adb251d93c59)

Use superconducting and normal labels only to the extent supported by the probes. A resistive intermediate regime may retain pairing or superconducting correlations, while zero resistance may follow a connected minority path. The same plotted boundary can have different meanings when it represents onset, a resistance fraction, a magnetic response or a thermodynamic anomaly. Paths assembled from different samples need explicit specimen labels. [R2](#ref-source-a1eeb97b9380bbe3) [R3](#ref-source-384b3a4f65f6493c) [R8](#ref-source-3504ee9027165d0a)

## Mathematical assumptions

An independent dimensionless example separates path geometry from mechanism. Define an imposed transition curve $T_c(x)=\max[0,1+v-(x^2-1)^2]$, and label a point superconducting when $0\lt T\lt T_c(x)$. The variable $x$ is a control coordinate, not an assigned pressure in GPa. With $v=0.2$, both peaks reach 1.2 and the valley remains at 0.2. A horizontal scan at $T=0.6$ crosses two superconducting intervals; at $T=0.1$ it crosses one connected interval. The two higher-temperature pockets therefore belong to one connected region in the full positive-temperature plane. [R4](#ref-source-9be3bcd270b306a2) [R5](#ref-source-da35691a947db0c5)

For $v=-0.2$, the imposed curve has a zero-temperature gap between two lobes. At $T=0.1$, its superconducting intervals run approximately from -1.355 to -0.404 and from 0.404 to 1.355. The analytic endpoints follow from $(x^2-1)^2=1+v-T$. Six cases, including scans above both peaks, agree with 120,000 independent grid evaluations. This construction demonstrates a logical distinction; it does not infer a second order parameter, pairing symmetry or microscopic interaction from the number of lobes. [R4](#ref-source-9be3bcd270b306a2)

A separate homogeneous example represents the historical temperature sequence. Let $f=a(T)s^2+s^4/2$, where $s\ge0$ and $a(T)=(T-1)(T-3)$ in dimensionless units. Its global minimum has $s^2=\max[0,-a(T)]$. Cooling through $T=4,2,0.5$ gives normal, superconducting and normal states. Only one superconducting interval exists. The model has continuous boundaries and no domains or hysteresis, so it cannot reproduce the observed history dependence of ErRh4B4. It illustrates which state reenters, not that material's magnetic mechanism. [R1](#ref-source-435b794ed32c7604) [R2](#ref-source-a1eeb97b9380bbe3)

## Experimental criteria

Sinha and colleagues' 1982 ErRh4B4 study combines neutron diffraction and resistivity. Its abstract reports an intermediate region below 1.2 K with ferromagnetic and modulated magnetic structures, followed below 0.71 K by disappearance of the modulation and superconductivity into a normal ferromagnetic state. The proposed coexistence of ferromagnetic and superconducting domains is an interpretation of these measurements, not proof of uniform coexistence at every microscopic position. The full historical methods remain unchecked here. [R1](#ref-source-435b794ed32c7604)

Prozorov and colleagues later resolve directional and historical differences using dynamic susceptibility. Their approximately 10 MHz probe uses a small excitation field, and their Figure 3 compares multiple temperature-ramp rates. Warming from the ferromagnetic state produces steps; cooling is more gradual. The reported rate insensitivity covers the tested ramps, not every possible relaxation timescale. Superconducting screening can conceal remaining ferromagnetic regions. Minor temperature loops also differ from the initial warming path, so sweep direction alone does not completely specify sample history. [R2](#ref-source-a1eeb97b9380bbe3)

Crespo and colleagues add local tunneling spectroscopy with a superconducting Pb tip. Their analysis deconvolves the tip response to infer the sample density of states. They select about twenty suitable surface locations; many other areas have suppressed or absent superconducting features. The representative heating and cooling transitions differ by about 90 mK. At 0.3 K the measured superconducting features come from the tip, while the inferred sample spectrum is featureless. This is a local, surface-sensitive absence within the measured conditions, not a complete volume census. The author manuscript identifies the crystal as the one previously used in the neutron and transport study. [R3](#ref-source-384b3a4f65f6493c)

Yuan and colleagues' original 2003 abstract reports disconnected pressure domes in partially Ge-substituted CeCu2Si2. Their later institutional report distinguishes the continuous region of the pure compound from the separated domes near substitution x=0.1. The comparison uses relative pressure $\Delta p=p-p_{c1}(x)$; the reference pressure depends on composition. Ge substitution changes both disorder and lattice volume. The report also combines comparison curves from different probes and uses distinct pressure-cell techniques across its range. These conditions prevent a universal pressure window from being assigned to every CeCu2(Si,Ge)2 sample. [R4](#ref-source-9be3bcd270b306a2) [R5](#ref-source-da35691a947db0c5)

URhGe and Eu-rich Sm/Ca/Eu nickelates provide field-scan examples, with different magnetic settings. The former has a second pocket near a b-axis spin reorientation; the latter combines selected high-field zero-resistance and screening observations. Keep these examples distinct from the normal-state reentrance on cooling in ErRh4B4. Similar phase-diagram shapes do not establish a shared microscopic mechanism. The sample-specific field and temperature conditions are treated in the linked field-induced-superconductivity page. ## Limits and disputes

A lowest measured temperature is a detection boundary, not proof that the true transition temperature vanishes. The institutional pressure report describes measurements extending down to 50 mK; its absence of observed superconductivity cannot establish an exact zero-temperature interval without further evidence. Likewise, its discussion of a possible volume collapse cites diffraction on CeCu2Ge2 rather than a direct structural measurement on every specimen in its superconducting phase diagram. Magnetic and valence-mediated pairing remain interpretations to test against additional measurements. [R5](#ref-source-da35691a947db0c5)

Complete scientific acceptance remains open. The original Sinha and Science pressure papers have only abstract-level checks here; other selected passages and figures have more detailed review. Raw spectra, absolute magnetic calibration, complete final-version comparisons and historical field boundaries remain incomplete. Local gaplessness, resistance, screening and global phase coherence must not be treated as interchangeable observations. The independent examples verify their stated assumptions only. ## Discovery implications

Record the full path, sample history, parameter uncertainties, measurement floor and transition criterion with each reported pocket. Compare warming, cooling and partial loops before assigning an equilibrium boundary. Separate a new region in a measured slice from a distinct phase in the full diagram, then use magnetic, structural and spectroscopic measurements to test the proposed mechanism. Preserve unresolved differences instead of averaging incompatible temperatures or pressure origins.
