## Scope

Electronic nematic order breaks a crystal's rotational symmetry without requiring a new translation period. This entry concerns the associated fluctuations as a candidate pairing interaction; observing static anisotropy is a different question. Spin, orbital and lattice responses can couple, so the name of a measured response does not identify its microscopic origin. The Fernandes–Chubukov–Schmalian review provides background on that distinction. [R1](#ref-source-bd2e7b2930bdd775)

Lederer and colleagues study a weakly coupled metal near an Ising nematic quantum critical point. Their controlled regime excludes a narrow region closest to criticality. Nematic fluctuations can enhance several pairing channels, with the background interaction often selecting the leading symmetry. This conditional result is not a universal transition-temperature law for iron compounds, cuprates or nickelates. [R4](#ref-source-24dd77640a555a0f)

## Mathematical assumptions

A transparent static illustration uses dimensionless electronic order $\phi$ and symmetry-matched strain $\epsilon$. Let the quadratic free-energy density be

$$F=\frac{r}{2}\phi^2+\frac{C}{2}\epsilon^2-g\phi\epsilon-h\phi-s\epsilon.$$

Here $h$ is a source conjugate to electronic order and $s$ is the corresponding stress. With this normalization, $r,C,g,h,s$ have units of energy density. The symmetric phase is locally stable when $r>0$, $C>0$ and $rC>g^2$. This is a uniform, equilibrium, small-response model; it omits spatial dynamics, domains and critical fluctuations. It makes the boundary-condition distinction in the Chu manuscript explicit. [R2](#ref-source-d29c3c764d26edfd)

At fixed strain, $\partial\phi/\partial h=1/r$ and $\partial\phi/\partial\epsilon=g/r$. Allowing strain to relax at fixed stress instead gives

$$\left.\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial h}\right|_s=\frac{C}{rC-g^2},\qquad\left.\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial s}\right|_h=\frac{g}{rC-g^2},\qquad C_{\mathrm{eff}}=C-\frac{g^2}{r}.$$

These derivatives are not interchangeable susceptibilities. Independent minimization checks all four parameter examples. A measured resistance anisotropy can have a linear proxy form $N=c\phi+b\epsilon$, giving $dN/d\epsilon=cg/r+b$ in the clamped electronic-response model. Conversion to electronic susceptibility therefore needs a proxy coefficient and background assumptions, not just the observation of a large resistance slope.

Adding a stabilizing $u\phi^4/4$ term changes the strain response about a finite biased state to $g/(r+3u\phi_0^2)$ for $u>0$. Three independent nonlinear-solution checks recover this derivative. Residual strain can round a response; this illustration does not calibrate a piezo stack or determine a material's critical temperature.

For the pairing problem, the nematic form factor is not the gap function. In Lederer's model, the induced vertex contains the squared magnitude of a nematic form factor and a susceptibility concentrated near zero momentum transfer. Its sign, normalization and frequency window belong to that model. Selected published equations and the author manuscript were checked; the full microscopic calculation was not reproduced. [R4](#ref-source-24dd77640a555a0f)

An independent circular-Fermi-surface illustration takes $f(\theta)=\cos2\theta$ and a positive forward-limit correction $\delta\lambda=\eta\langle f^2|\Delta|^2\rangle$, with dimensionless $\eta>0$ and normalized trial functions. For $\Delta=1,\sqrt2\cos2\theta,\sqrt2\sin2\theta,\sqrt2\cos\theta$, direct quadrature gives overlaps $1/2,3/4,1/4,1/2$. Several channels are enhanced by the same nematic form factor. These illustrative overlaps exclude finite-width mixing, self-energy and lattice anisotropy; they neither establish the winning material gap nor yield an experimental transition temperature.

## Experimental criteria

Chu and colleagues measured strain-induced resistance changes in Co-substituted BaFe2As2. Selected author-manuscript methods distinguish controlled strain from controlled stress and check strain transmission. A PubMed record flags a later correction; its publisher text remains unavailable in this review, so final-version reconciliation stays open. No numerical phase boundary is extracted here. [R2](#ref-source-d29c3c764d26edfd) [R6](#ref-source-052b3d26e6f02b35)

Kuo and colleagues' earlier manuscript studies differential elastoresistance in five optimally doped iron-based families, including FeTeSe. P-substituted BaFe2As2 follows Curie–Weiss behavior over a wider measured temperature interval; several other samples deviate at lower temperature. Thus the cited study already contains chalcogenide evidence, but it does not establish universal behavior across every composition. Its B2g label uses stated tetragonal axes; rotating axis conventions changes symmetry labels. Exact composition reconciliation with the final paper remains pending. [R3](#ref-source-f9ae22fe4f6ed654)

Romero and colleagues' earlier manuscript measures complex THz conductivity of FeTeSe films and fits parallel conduction channels. It discusses a qualitative resemblance to nematic-fluctuation models. Conductivity and a successful channel fit are not direct measurements of the Cooper-pairing vertex; the manuscript-to-final-article comparison remains incomplete. [R5](#ref-source-05b2d5ff6757b5eb)

For each experiment retain the strain component, coordinate system, stress or strain boundary condition, transmission calibration, thermal-contraction offset and linear-response window. Separate symmetry-breaking response from isotropic background. Compare elastoresistance, Raman and elastic measurements only after stating their frequency, momentum, lattice-coupling and proxy assumptions. Matching trends do not make different response functions identical.

## Limits and disputes

A Curie–Weiss extrapolation defines a model-dependent temperature scale. It need not be the measured structural transition, and neither is automatically the superconducting transition. Disorder, finite bias, competing magnetism and changing transport coefficients can affect the inference. The static illustrative free energy above contains no dynamical pairing kernel and therefore cannot prove that the measured fluctuations cause superconductivity.

A small-momentum interaction can be an amplifier without uniquely fixing gap sign. Near degeneracy, even a modest channel-dependent correction can change the leading state; away from degeneracy, background interactions may keep their ordering. Strong critical feedback can invalidate the controlled approximation. A complete comparison must include the normal self-energy as well as the anomalous pairing interaction.

## Discovery implications

Use a candidate nematic response to design matched symmetry-resolved measurements and competing-channel calculations. Ask whether one model explains gap anisotropy, normal-state response and tuning trends on comparable samples. Nickelates remain an open target for such tests; an iron-based mechanism cannot simply be assigned to them. Preserve evidence against the hypothesis alongside supporting correlations, and distinguish a scoped source check from full scientific acceptance.
