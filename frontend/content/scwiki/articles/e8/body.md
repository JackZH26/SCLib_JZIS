## Scope

Dimensionality compares geometry with physical length scales. A layered crystal, a two-dimensional electronic band and a superconducting film with a nearly uniform order parameter through its thickness are different descriptions. Compare thickness with the perpendicular coherence length, wire transverse dimensions with coherence lengths, and lateral size with magnetic screening lengths. Interlayer coupling, substrate, disorder and carrier density can change when layers are removed. Reduced thickness alone does not establish an ideal two-dimensional phase transition. [R1](#ref-source-408113812912d816) [R6](#ref-source-c80fb39cdf748a83)

For a short-range two-dimensional phase model, vortex–antivortex unbinding separates quasi-long-range order from a disordered phase. The relevant stiffness is renormalized by fluctuations. In a charged film, magnetic screening changes the vortex interaction at long distance: Kosterlitz and Thouless explicitly identified this limitation in their original paper. BKT-like behavior requires an appropriate range of length, current and temperature scales; do not transfer the neutral infinite-system limit to every finite superconducting sheet. [R1](#ref-source-408113812912d816) [R2](#ref-source-8f81510a80c35e3a)

## Mathematical assumptions

Define the sheet phase stiffness $J$ in energy units by $F_\theta=(J/2)\int d^2r\,|\nabla\theta|^2$. A single vortex has logarithmic energy $\pi J\ln(L/a)$ and positional entropy $2k_B\ln(L/a)$ within this model, where $a$ is a core cutoff. The universal jump is $J_R(T_{\rm BKT}^-)=2k_BT_{\rm BKT}/\pi$, with the long-distance **renormalized** stiffness $J_R$. The elementary energy–entropy balance explains the scale; it is not a replacement for the renormalization calculation. For a local sheet inductance $L_k$ per square, the corresponding stiffness convention is $J=\hbar^2/(4e^2L_k)$. Frequency, vortex response and finite size matter when comparing a measured inductance with the static jump. [R1](#ref-source-408113812912d816) [R2](#ref-source-8f81510a80c35e3a)

In a uniform narrow wire near its mean-field transition, the zero-current Ginzburg–Landau saddle gives $\Delta F_0=(8\sqrt2/3)u_c A\xi$, where $u_c=B_c^2/(2\mu_0)$ is the SI condensation-energy density and $A$ is cross-sectional area. A local check integrates the normalized saddle $f(y)=\tanh(y/\sqrt2)$, $y=x/\xi$, in $\int[(1-f^2)^2+2(df/dy)^2]dy$. This checks the barrier factor under those assumptions, not a finite-current barrier, quantum tunneling action or the full LAMH prefactor. The original Langer–Ambegaokar and McCumber–Halperin full derivations have not been retrieved for this review. [R3](#ref-source-aa12d674a498e02d) [R4](#ref-source-866d4492c91f5b39)

For rare independent thermal phase slips, define $r_0$ explicitly as the zero-bias rate **per direction**. With $\Phi_0=h/(2e)$, detailed balance gives $r_\pm=r_0\exp[\pm I\Phi_0/(2k_BT)]$ to leading small-bias barrier tilt, $V=\Phi_0(r_+-r_-)$ and $R_{I\to0}=\Phi_0^2r_0/(k_BT)$. The thermal activation factor belongs inside $r_0$; this definition avoids confusing a total event rate with a directional one. Low-temperature resistance saturation alone does not identify coherent quantum phase slips. [R3](#ref-source-aa12d674a498e02d) [R4](#ref-source-866d4492c91f5b39) [R8](#ref-source-42e0dec220b7b3ea)

## Experimental criteria

Xi's encapsulated NbSe2 devices distinguish several transition definitions. In the checked author manuscript, the monolayer Aslamazov–Larkin fit gives $T_{c0}\approx3.0$ K, close to the 50% normal-resistance criterion; the inferred BKT transition lies near the 1% criterion. Those are separate quantities. Normal resistance was taken at 8 K. The four-terminal field series used a 50% criterion, while a separate two-terminal monolayer device used a rapid-resistance-drop criterion. The 0.3 K measurement floor is not another transition. Large in-plane critical fields support the authors' Ising-pairing interpretation under their model, rather than proving triplet pairing by themselves. The journal issue is from 2016; online publication was 9 November 2015. [R6](#ref-source-c80fb39cdf748a83)

Bezryadin's nanotube-templated amorphous Mo79Ge21 wires were approximately 10 nm or less in diameter and 95–185 nm long. The resistance drop near 5.5 K belongs to the electrodes, after which the wire resistance could be isolated. In the studied series, samples on opposite sides of a normal-resistance scale near $R_Q=h/(4e^2)\approx6.45$ kilohms showed decreasing or increasing resistance on cooling. One superconducting sample was checked down to 50 mK. These are sample-specific transport observations and a quantum-fluctuation interpretation, not a universal wire transition temperature or universal critical resistance for all geometries and environments. [R7](#ref-source-9ebba9ba0616123e)

Astafiev's InOx loop experiment used microwave spectroscopy to investigate coherent coupling between flux states. The checked author version describes a 35 nm film, a narrow segment about 40 nm wide and 400 nm long, and measurements at 40 mK. A two-state fit gave $E_S/h=4.9$ GHz and persistent current 24 nA for the illustrated device; resonator features distorted portions of the spectrum. The film's separately reported 2.7 K transition is distinct from the loop spectroscopy temperature. Coherent coupling evidence is stronger and different in kind from fitting a dissipative resistance tail; the selected readings do not reproduce the complete supplementary analysis. [R8](#ref-source-42e0dec220b7b3ea)

For aluminum nanoparticles, Black, Ralph and Tinkham reported even/odd differences in tunneling excitation thresholds and field suppression of a spectroscopic gap in sufficiently large particles, with radius roughly 5 nm or greater in their abstract. Radius is not diameter. Discrete levels, charging and parity enter the interpretation. Local pairing signatures do not demonstrate zero resistance across an isolated grain or global phase coherence in an array. This entry currently relies on the original publisher abstract, without a complete original-text audit. [R9](#ref-source-9fd1a118a8a9914b)

## Limits and disputes

Use several diagnostics for a BKT interpretation: nonlinear current–voltage behavior, stiffness and resistance scaling over justified windows, together with finite-size, heating and inhomogeneity checks. A convenient fit does not establish its asymptotic assumptions. Likewise, Giordano's early indium-wire work reported low-temperature deviations from thermal-activation predictions consistent with quantum tunneling; it should not be generalized into a rule that every residual resistance tail proves quantum phase slips. Weak links, contacts, normal regions and electromagnetic environment need independent controls. ## Discovery implications

Build thickness and width series with documented oxidation protection, substrate, contacts and disorder. Store pairing-fit, phase-coherence, resistance and local-spectroscopy criteria separately, retaining the actual sample and measurement temperature. A route that strengthens pairing can simultaneously reduce phase stiffness; dimensional reduction is a tuning parameter, not a guarantee of a higher usable superconducting transition.
